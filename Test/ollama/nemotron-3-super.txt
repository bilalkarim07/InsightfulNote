- Independent benchmark results for the model **have not yet been published**.  
- Journalists covering the story are **currently reporting only the company’s claims** (i.e., they have not cited any third‑party test data).

**2. Claims (statements that require verification)**  
- The assertion that the new model is “significantly better” than the prior version in the three domains (coding, reasoning, mathematics).  
- Any implication that the improvement is objective, measurable, or universally applicable without supporting data.  
- The suggestion that the model’s performance advantage is established fact rather than the company’s own assessment.

| Item | Why it matters | How to verify |
|------|----------------|---------------|
| **Benchmark scores or performance metrics** (e.g., HumanEval, GSM‑8K, MATH) for both the new and prior models | Determines whether the claimed superiority is demonstrable | Request or locate independent test results; if unavailable, note the absence |
| **Testing methodology** (prompt sets, temperature, sampling, number of runs) | Ensures comparability and rules out cherry‑picking | Ask the company for detailed protocol or seek replication by a third party |

| **Model specifications** (size, training data, compute used) | Contextualizes performance gains and helps assess plausibility | Review the company’s release notes, model card, or technical paper |
| **Any caveats or limitations disclosed by the company** | Prevents overstating capabilities | Examine the announcement for disclosed weaknesses or scope limits |

- **Lead with attribution**: “The company announced a new AI model and said it outperforms its predecessor on coding, reasoning, and math tasks.”  
- **Qualify the claim**: Use phrases such as “according to the company,” “the company claims,” or “the firm states.”  
- **Highlight the lack of independent verification**: Follow the claim with a sentence like “Independent benchmark results have not yet been released, and no third‑party tests have been publicly shared at this time.”  
- **Provide context**: Explain what “significantly better” would typically mean (e.g., percentage point gains on standard benchmarks) and note that without data the magnitude remains unknown.  
- **Include expert caution**: If possible, quote a neutral analyst or researcher who stresses the need for peer‑reviewed evaluation before accepting performance claims.  
- **Avoid definitive language**: Do not write “the model is better” or “the model surpasses the old version”; instead, keep the statement tentative (“the company says it is better”).  
- **Update mechanism**: Note that the story will be revised as soon as independent benchmarks become available, signaling to readers that the current assessment is provisional.  

By structuring the piece around sourced statements, clearly marking what is unverified, and refraining from presenting the company’s superiority claim as established fact, Newsroom adheres to journalistic standards of accuracy and neutrality while still informing readers of the announcement.

"An AI startup claims its new model is 10x better than GPT-class

or detailed results."

Explain how Newsroom should approach this story.

The goal is to inform readers without amplifying an unsupported claim.

RESPONSE:
**How Newsroom Should Approach the Story**

1. **Verify the Claim’s Basis**  
   - Ask the startup for the specific benchmark(s) they used, the test environment, and any raw results.  
   - If they cannot or will not share methodology, note that the claim lacks verifiable evidence.

2. **Seek Independent Confirmation**  
   - Reach out to neutral AI researchers or labs that specialize in model evaluation (e.g., Stanford HAI, Allen Institute, MLCommons).  
   - Ask whether they can reproduce the alleged 10× improvement using publicly available details or standard benchmarks (e.g., MMLU, GSM‑8K, HumanEval).

3. **Provide Context**  
   - Explain what “GPT‑class models” currently refers to (e.g., GPT‑4, GPT‑3.5) and typical performance ranges on major benchmarks.  
   - Clarify that a 10× gain would be extraordinary and would normally be accompanied by peer‑reviewed papers or reproducible code.

4. **Frame the Story Around Uncertainty**  
   - Lead with what is known: the startup *claims* a 10× improvement but has not disclosed methodology or allowed independent testing.  
   - Emphasize the absence of corroborating data rather than repeating the claim as fact.

5. **Avoid Amplification**  
   - Do not repeat the exact “10× better” phrasing in headlines or promotional copy unless it is clearly qualified as an unverified claim.  
   - Use language such as “the startup says” or “according to the company’s statement,” and pair it with a note about missing evidence.

6. **Offer Paths Forward**  
   - Indicate what would be needed to substantiate the claim (open‑source code, detailed benchmark logs, third‑party audit).  
   - Encourage readers to stay skeptical until such evidence appears.
